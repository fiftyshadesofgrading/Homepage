Two ideas caught my eye as I was scrolling through my LinkedIn feed. One was a thoughtful post, with an equally opposite comment posted an hour later. It was a rare LinkedIn ratio'd situation.  

The first was someone proud of their AutoCAD skills, mostly 2D structural work, and making the case that the skills still count. The second was a reply saying those tools are done: they hadn't drawn anything in AutoCAD or Revit in years, and soon we're all just going to code. This user went on to tout their proficiency in developing  model geometry through the use of AI prompts. 

My first reaction was to pick a side. This is the internet - and LinkedIn after all! My second reaction, after I'd stopped being mad at my phone, was that **they're both right**, and the argument only exists because each one is looking at a different part of the job.

## Nobody wants you to one-shot a building

The "we'll all just code" hot-take envisions a design as something you generate: feed in the rules and the constraints, and get out the model, done. Aspects of the work really are heading that way, and I'm all for it! I've spend a lot of my free time developing scripts for Civil 3D and AutoCAD for exactly that reason.

But our clients don't buy a generated model. They buy a design that they understood, argued about, and signed off on; one decision at a time. That back and forth happens on drawings: a plan, a section, a detail with a dimension someone circled in red. The sheet is how engineers and clients talk to each other, and it is also what the permit reviewer, the contractor and the inspector work from. A perfect model you can't explain in a sheet set is just an expensive guess.

In my own land development days, the thing that got a project through was never just the model. It was the plan set the town's reviewer marked up in red and sent back, and the one I sent back to them. That sheet set was the conversation between me, the client and the approving agency. I tell my Civil 3D students that modeling and design is one half of the job, the other half is communication - be that through construction plans and sections, renderings, VR/AR, or reports. The possibilities for communication are limitless with the advancements of our design technology, but communicating our designs will always be fundamental to our industries. 

## The niche work is still very much a drawing

A lot of the industry doesn't live in tidy parametric families. Retrofit details for a 1920s building. A one-off connection on a pedestrian bridge. A grading plan where the existing ground is whatever the survey crew collected. The details that make these work are drawn by someone who knows what matters and what to leave out. That's a drafting skill, and it's an engineering skill wearing a drafting hat.

So yes, the person proud of their 2D chops has something real. Knowing how to communicate a design clearly on a sheet doesn't go out of date when the tools change, any more than knowing how to write did when we stopped using typewriters.

## And the coder isn't wrong either

The other half of the job really is changing. Clicking the same 40 commands to set up every sheet, fixing layer names by hand, re-labeling a profile because the alignment moved: that work should be automated, and more and more of it is. Scripts, the API, Dynamo, APS, and AI that writes the boring parts. If drafting by hand is the only thing you know, that half of the job is coming for you.

## Both, not either

The future I'd bet on isn't "drafters vs. coders." It's people who can do both:

- **Draft well enough to know what a good sheet or CAD model looks like**, because that's what the client, the reviewer and the field actually use.
- **Code well enough to stop doing it by hand**, so the hours go into the design instead of the clicking.
- **Know which is which.** Automate the repeatable, draw the judgment calls.

The tools will keep changing. Being able to explain a design to the people paying for it won't.
