Two posts went by on my feed this week, pretty much back to back.

The first was someone proud of their AutoCAD skills, mostly 2D structural work, and making the case that the skill still counts. The second was a reply saying those tools are done: they hadn't drawn anything in AutoCAD or Revit in years, and soon we're all just going to code.

My first reaction was to pick a side. My second reaction, after I'd stopped being mad at my phone, was that **they're both right**, and the argument only exists because each one is looking at a different part of the job.

## Nobody wants you to one-shot a building

The "we'll all just code" take pictures a design as something you generate: feed in the rules, get out the model, done. Parts of the work really are heading that way, and I'm all for it. I spend a lot of my free time scripting Civil 3D and AutoCAD for exactly that reason.

But clients don't buy a generated model. They buy a design they understood, argued with, and signed off on, one decision at a time. That back and forth happens on drawings: a plan, a section, a detail with a dimension someone circled in red. The sheet is how engineers and clients talk to each other, and it's also what the permit reviewer, the contractor and the inspector work from. A perfect model you can't explain in a sheet set is just an expensive guess.

## The niche work is still very much a drawing

A lot of the industry doesn't live in tidy parametric families. Retrofit details for a 1920s building. A one-off connection on a pedestrian bridge. A grading plan where the existing ground is whatever the survey crew found. The details that make these work are drawn by someone who knows what matters and what to leave out. That's a drafting skill, and it's an engineering skill wearing a drafting hat.

So yes, the person proud of their 2D chops has something real. Knowing how to communicate a design clearly on a sheet doesn't go out of date when the tools change, any more than knowing how to write did when we stopped using typewriters.

## And the coder isn't wrong either

The other half of the job really is changing. Clicking the same 40 commands to set up every sheet, fixing layer names by hand, re-labeling a profile because the alignment moved: that work should be automated, and more and more of it is. Scripts, the API, Dynamo, APS, AI that writes the boring parts. If drafting by hand is the only thing you know, that half of the job is coming for you.

## Both, not either

The future I'd bet on isn't "drafters vs. coders." It's people who can do both:

- **Draft well enough to know what a good sheet looks like**, because that's what the client, the reviewer and the field actually use.
- **Code well enough to stop doing it by hand**, so the hours go into the design instead of the clicking.
- **Know which is which.** Automate the repeatable, draw the judgment calls.

The tools will keep changing. Being able to explain a design to the people paying for it won't.
